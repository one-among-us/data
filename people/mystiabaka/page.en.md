## How We Met

Speaking of Mystia… when I first got to know her, I had just started getting involved in community support work. At the time, I was handling a domestic violence case, and she happened to reach out to me for help because she was being abused at home. By chance, we got to know each other on Twitter.

Although she was younger than me, she seemed more mature than I was. And since she seemed to be doing relatively well back then, I got into the habit of calling her “Sis.”

She, of course, didn’t take that lying down, and loved to call me sis right back:

“I’ll always love this world, because now I have a super‑duper amazing sister!”

Back then, I wasn’t yet formally involved in social work, so I didn’t have very strict professional boundaries. So, during the summer of 2024, when her parents happened to be away on a business trip, I travelled to Beijing to meet her in person.

On the way to Beijing, she chatted with me about cooking.

> “Sis Nuomi, what kind of food do you usually like to eat?”
>
> “Actually, I brought a bundle of noodles from my hometown. When we meet, I’ll cook some for you~”
>
> “Sounds great, I’d love that.”

## Summer Dream in Beijing

When the train pulled into the station, I hurried from the platform to the exit with my luggage, and she was already waiting for me in a corner as promised. She was as quiet as a delicate little flower, light and airy, and seemed a bit shy. That kind of presence made her easy to spot in the crowd.

After we met up, we decided to go to the nearby Saizeriya for a meal. After all, Saizeriya is cheap and tasty—everyone agrees it’s a top choice for a casual get‑together.

At the table, we first talked about her family situation, and then I asked her about her plans for the future.

> “Hmm… the future…? Well, I just need to get through high school first. As for after the college entrance exams, maybe I’ll go to Zhejiang…? Or the Pearl River Delta for university.”

Mystia was a girl who held hope and dreams for the future. She believed that no matter how bleak things were now, the future would be brighter.

At least, that was the case back then…

It was no surprise, then, that this girl had a sweet tooth in private. She’d buy fermented cacao beans and make chocolate herself in the oven—though the chocolates she made were usually reserved especially for me. She could also cook all sorts of home‑style dishes, including her favourite eel rice. She was a little culinary genius.

Remember that bundle of noodles I mentioned I brought on the train? They’re a type of noodle from my hometown of Yongkang, called “tusu noodles.” They taste excellent, but are quite tricky to cook. My grandmother used to run a restaurant, so I naturally learned the art of cooking these noodles from her.

I showed her how to do it: cut the pork into small pieces, separating the fat from the lean. Heat the wok, render the fat pieces until some of the fat has cooked out, then add the lean meat and stir‑fry until cooked. Remove both from the wok, add some soy sauce and hot water. Following the traditional method, shake the noodles into the boiling water, let the steam rise, scoop them into bowls, finally drizzle the rendered pork fat over the top—and two large bowls of soup noodles with delicate white strands were ready.

“Sis Nuomi, your noodles are really good,” Mystia said, full of praise.

I smiled and affectionately ruffled her hair.

But to my surprise, by the next meal, she was able to replicate the process perfectly. I had planned to cook noodles for her, but in the end, she cooked them for me. And the noodles she made tasted remarkably similar to my grandmother’s handiwork—which is to say, it required quite a bit of skill and finesse to reach that level.

On my first day in Beijing, we had four meals, three of which were noodles, and the other was Saizeriya.

Apart from Saizeriya, I hardly ate out at all during my time in Beijing. Thanks to her cooking, I didn’t have to resort to KFC or McDonald’s.

During my days in Beijing, she loved to pull me out of bed early in the morning for walks. I’m not really one for going out, but because she was by my side, I happily went along.

Beijing at six in the morning is cool and pleasant—even in the height of July, the breeze that greets you is refreshing. We’d sit on a bench by the lake and watch the sunrise together. The sunrise was beautiful, almost dreamlike. We’d stroll along the lakeside path, stopping now and then, as she chatted with me about ham radio and shared her plans for the future.

She said she wanted to go aerial filming at Yantian Port.

She said she wanted to spend a few years in Hangzhou.

She said…

She was clearly a girl with a clear vision for herself, full of dreams for the future, and mature beyond her years.

I think it was on my second day in Beijing that I sat on her balcony, staring out the window in a daze:

“Outside looks like a dream… it doesn’t feel real…”

“Sis Nuomi, this isn’t a dream. And even if it were, it’s a beautiful dream. Because you’re right here with me.”

I was so happy.

Back then, I could clearly tell she liked me, and later on too, though she never said it outright. After all, ethically speaking, a helper and the person being helped shouldn’t fall in love…

I stayed for a week and then left Beijing, promising that we’d meet again.

## Autumn Whispers in Hangzhou

Before National Day in 2024, I left school and went home three days early due to some circumstances. Mystia heard about it and managed to get permission to take time off from school, travelling south from Beijing to see me. But due to some unforeseen circumstances, my parents were planning to send me to a conversion institution again. After that, we changed our plans and headed to Hangzhou instead.

The train we took was the K1050, departing from Yongkang South Station at 03:28 on the Jinhua–Wenzhou railway—a route I knew well. But later, that train quietly disappeared amid changes to the railway timetable, like so many things in life that never came to pass. I will never know why.

We arrived in Hangzhou on National Day. We spent some time together, had desserts at Honeymoon Dessert—she ordered the golden mango sago with pomelo and mango pancake—and then went to Saizeriya again, chatting happily about all sorts of things. Truth be told, the Saizeriya in the Yangtze Delta doesn’t taste any different from the one in Beijing; what matters most is who you’re eating with.

After our brief reunion, I took the train back to Yongkang, and the next day I rushed off to Wuhan for a meeting.

I always held onto the promise of our next meeting.

But we were always so good at meeting, and never thought about parting.

## Together Through the Winter Snow

At that time, Mystia was in her first year of high school in Beijing. On one hand, she was a day student, so she faced abuse at home when she returned; on the other hand, her family treated her terribly, even refusing to pay her high school tuition fees, despite the school’s intervention. In the end, some other volunteers and I crowdfunded her tuition and medical insurance costs, and set aside some living expenses for her in case of emergencies.

Her school had wall‑mounted landline phones, and we’d call each other every day to chat. She’d tell me about interesting things that happened, or about subjects she was struggling with, and she’d pester me to teach her.

Back then, she loved writing poetry:

> A slanting sunbeam breaks the dusty pane,
> 
> The veiled glass hides a face I know in vain.
> 
> A gentle breeze just brushes past my hand,
> 
> Then in the mirror, clear, that person stands.
>
> 2024‑09‑12

I also remember the casual essays she wrote during boring moments at school, which she’d photograph and send to me when she got home.

> Winter in the snow country is long, so long that the people there are used to imagining spring’s arrival as farther away than it really is, and speaking of hope as more lightly than it deserves. Deep in their bones, there is a faint, indescribable stubbornness, along with a lingering poetry and sorrow.
>
> Though I am not there, I too am living through a long winter, watching for spring, watching for what lies ahead.
>
> 2024‑10‑12

“I’m thinking of writing a novel,” I said.

“Then could you include this little essay in your novel, Sis Nuomi?”

“Why?”

“Because if it’s in there, then if one day I’m not around, I can always be with you~”

…Such a quirky girl, full of clever ideas.

On weekends, she’d play Apex with me, and we’d chat on voice calls at night. We were both so happy back then, so happy.

Everything was still beautiful. Life was still life, and the future still looked bright—like a gem within easy reach.

…So close, yet only close.

Those happy times passed slowly, yet in the blink of an eye, they were gone.

The turning point came on November 23, 2024.

By then, her parents had completely cut her off financially, and she was relying on me and a few other volunteers for meals and daily necessities.

Her parents finally grew suspicious and demanded to know where the money was coming from. Then came the familiar scene of smashed belongings, shouting, and violence.

Helpless, she ran out of the house and called me, crying about what had happened.

> “Sis Nuomi… I don’t want to go home… I’m so tired…”

Later, we helped her rent a place in a slightly more remote part of Beijing, to at least give her some distance from her family for a while.

She moved out everything she could carry. During that time, life felt as beautiful as a dream to her.

After the final exams came the winter vacation. Her parents soon came knocking, demanding that she return home for the holidays, and despite our volunteers’ earnest attempts to persuade them, they wouldn’t budge.

And so the dream shattered. Helplessly, she moved her things back home and continued to suffer verbal and physical abuse. Her parents first forced her to cut her hair; when she refused, they beat her until she gave in.

In the end, consumed by despair, she took her own life on January 26. From the start of the holiday to that day, with both ends set aside, only six short days lay in between.

Just six days. Six days…

I don’t know what could have happened to make a child who once held so much hope for the future lose all hope in just six days, and choose to end her life…

> Sis Nuomi, I’m so tired. I don’t want to keep waiting anymore.
>
> Will the future really be okay?
>
> Sis Nuomi, please don’t let anything happen to you. Stay safe and sound.

I opened the copy of *Das Kapital (Revised Edition)* she left behind, and turned to the flyleaf.

These… are some of the few traces she left behind in this world.

> I have only seen that in Drama’s on TV  
> While such words are being thrown around  
> I wonder what she could possibly be thinking about right now  
> Somewhere far, far away  
> And I wanted to cry  
> I wanted to cry  
> A day that should’ve been so long is already ending  
> She lived, lived, lived, lived, lived  
> Lived, lived, lived, didn’t she[^1]  
>
> —Ikiteita nda yo na (Song: Aimyon)

I think back to that line from Mystia’s essay: “Though I am not there, I too am living through a long winter, watching for spring, watching for what lies ahead.” But she never even made it to the spring of the following year. She left on the eve of spring’s arrival, before her next birthday, alone in that harsh winter. Like Yoko in *Snow Country*, who falls like a stiff insect, her life cut short.

Beneath the eerie yet beautiful night sky interwoven with flames and the Milky Way, Komako cradles Yoko and lets out a pained scream, while Shimamura, overwhelmed, feels as if “the Milky Way came flooding into his heart.”

And now, as I look through the things she left behind, I feel a similar daze. The poems and essays she wrote, the books she read, the photos she took, and the words she once said to me—they still lie there quietly. But in the crowd, I can no longer find that light, airy, delicate little flower.

She never lingered long in the community, didn’t even leave behind a proper tweet. But I will always remember her. She really was a wonderful person.
