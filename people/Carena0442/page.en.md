> Dedicated to the memory of our late friend Carena, and to everything she brought into our lives[^1].
> 
> One year ago today marks the passing of Carena, a dear friend and someone I once helped rescue. I am deeply sorry that circumstances at the time prevented us from publishing an obituary, even though our side had prepared everything; so today, a year later, I am here to fulfill that promise.

I first met Carena back in China, in March 2021. At the time, due to family conflicts on the eve of her college entrance exam, she fled her home in Gansu and flew straight to Guangzhou. I was responsible for drafting statements to negotiate with her school and speak with her teacher. My first impression was that her initiative was surprisingly strong, though she could also be a bit emotional.
Later, the incident surrounding that rescue blew up online—most of you have probably heard bits and pieces of it, so I won’t go into detail here: Carena had had agreed to prepare for her exams in Guangzhou, but because the shelter provided there turned out to be completely unfit for studying—unfit even for basic living. This led to Carena having a mental breakdown in the middle of the night and leaving. Attempts to communicate failed, and when she was threatened with police reports, the two sides broke off completely.

I was present online through the entire process. Virtually everything the public knows about Carena, I was there to witness, and I helped her respond to many of the controversies. Attacking a victim in the name of helping them is never acceptable—that was the stance I made clear back then, and it remains my stance today. Once the conflict and internet heat died down, Carena stayed intermittently at her ex-partner’s place in Guangzhou, working part-time jobs while posing as a university student while preparing for the college exams. Whenever she ran out of food or money for transit, I lent her what little I could to get her through the immediate pinch. Everyone was fixated on the controversy of her breaking off with the rescuers, debating whether the assistance was reasonable or fair, yet hardly anyone cared about what happened next. After a failed rescue left behind a shattered mess, the person at the centre of it still had to go on living.

…I still remember that one night when she suddenly called me out of the blue. On the other end of the line late at night, she was laughing hysterically, her speech barely intelligible. In a rare, extraordinarily bright tone, she called me “Sis Emma,” incoherently repeating that she loved us, liked everyone, and that everything was fine. I knew this was mostly the effect of overdosage, but she was happy in that moment—so happy that even through her chaotic phrasing, you could still sense her simple desire to call and share that feeling with me. I stayed on the line with her for fifteen or twenty minutes before she said she wouldn’t bother me any longer, hanging up to enjoy the fleeting peace granted by the substances. Her life in Guangzhou back then was anything but peaceful—she was often without a fixed place to stay, floating between part-time shifts and restaurant work, living hand-to-mouth. Yet she stubbornly refused to take too much help from me; I recall that all the money she borrowed from me in Guangzhou didn’t even add up to the price of a couple of flight tickets she took.

I don’t know when she started using drugs or overdosing. I was worried at first, but once I realized her depression was no safer even when she wasn’t using, I simply reminded her repeatedly to take care of herself. That is probably why, as a former rescuer, I maintained a good relationship with Carena: because I never tried, nor ever wanted, to impose my will on her. I respected her independent agency as an individual—until her passing, and through the one year, ten years, and endless years since.

Given her turbulent living condition before the exam, the college entrance exam naturally didn’t go well. The rare moments when she was accidentally sober after an OD—and not depressed—were almost entirely spent on work and basic survival, leaving her no time to ponder her future. On top of that, her family altered her university application choices without her consent after the exam. And so she repeated the grade; and so she prepared for the exam once more, spending that period confronting her family and school on one front, while relying on intermittent ODs to drag herself through a stretch of time she might not have even wanted to live through in the first place. During this time, Carena entered another intimate relationship—it began quite by chance but developed rapidly. The other person happens to be a close friend of mine with whom I remain in touch to this day. At the time, I was happy for them and helped them on multiple occasions (both financial support and emotional backup), but things ultimately did not end well. Out of respect for the other person’s privacy, I won’t elaborate on this relationship or the tragedy involved. I only want to mention one detail: in April of her repeat year (2022), when this friend was hospitalized, Carena left home once again and travelled through multiple setbacks just to care for them locally. A person who would be suspended and sent home by her school over an OD was nevertheless capable of responding so swiftly and effectively when her partner was in distress. It reminded me once more that when it came to her own life and future, she refused to submit; whenever she genuinely wanted to do something, no matter how others tried to stop her, she would do it—with fierce resolve and formidable efficiency. That was Carena’s very essence: at once completely indifferent yet fiercely resilient; shedding everything under the crushing weight of duty with contempt, yet throwing everything she had into the things she cherished. Heavy or light, it was all decided in a single thought, unbothered by public chatter, and completely unmoored from the regulations of worldly logic or so-called “common sense.”

Despite dropping out of school, enduring abuse from her family, and travelling across provinces to care for her partner, Carena finally completed her repeat exam in 2022 and was admitted to East China University of Science and Technology, majoring in chemistry. I was truly happy for her then, and had already helped prepare everything for her and her partner to move to Shanghai. Her family agreed to support her studying abroad after graduation, on the condition that she complete her undergraduate degree according to their demands. That summer, even though everything seemed fine on the surface, Carena’s mood fluctuated wildly—a reflection, perhaps, of how she always treated major crises with nonchalance, yet grew panicked and exhausted by the mundanity of everyday life.

On one occasion, she climbed up high with the intention of ending her life. After chatting with me to calm down, she realized she couldn’t get back down the way she came. I drew diagrams and demonstrated how to hang off the edge of the platform to drop down safely, but in the end, I had to call emergency services for a rescue—without, of course, revealing the true reason she had gone up there, claiming instead that she just wanted to see if she could climb up and didn’t realize how hard it would be to get down.

Another time, overwhelmed by anxiety about college life, she walked onto the rooftop. I told her she didn’t have to handle anything that felt too heavy. If planning the trip or buying school supplies stressed her out, I could handle the entire process for her; even if she just laid paralyzed in bed, nobody would—or could—blame her, so long as everything was arranged in the end. During the call, Carena repeatedly threatened to break off contact or end her life immediately, likely terrified that I would use the help I had given her during her re-enrollment (including study aids and essay edits) to morally blackmail her. Only after I made it clear that I had no such intention—and that threats wouldn’t work on me, did she shift her focus to opposing my offer to temporarily carry that burden for her.
I knew her panic was likely temporary, but that offer wasn’t just to let her know she could step back when responsibility felt unbearable (to stabilize her and ease suicidal urges); it was also to show her that stepping away could be done safely without destroying her life—as long as she found a reliable friend to hold things together while she was unstable. In the end, Carena took my advice, though she didn’t leave everything to me; after all, she was only panicked by the rigid expectations forced upon her, not incapable of carrying them.

Once at university, Carena was naturally not the conventional “model student,” eventually prompting the university to demand her medical leave on account of her mental health. With the COVID-19 pandemic still ongoing, things became exceptionally difficult. When her parents arrived at the school’s request, their response was utterly absurd—actively pushing for her suspension, berating Carena at every turn, to the point where even the conservative university officials called them out for showing zero respect for their child’s mental state. During this time, Carena was forced to cut her hair short because long hair “made her look like a lunatic”—an ordeal both infuriating and ridiculous. I spent those days helping Carena negotiate with the school while simultaneously looking after her brokenhearted and distressed partner. After all, I had grown accustomed to Carena’s volatility, standing by her even before her own family could accept her (her parents were still complaining at the time: “She was at a good university, how can she just stop going?” while threatening to drag her into a psychiatric hospital again). For Carena, the shift between warmth and hostility happened in a heartbeat; anything that forced her to sustain long-term responsibility after an initial wave of passion invited immediate burnout. She was capable of bearing heavy responsibilities, but only on the condition that she chose them freely.

The White Paper protests. Carena took to the streets, supporting classmates who cannot be named here. Her roommate was terrible both as a person and academically, threatening during the protests to have Carena and other protesters killed, while later mindlessly celebrating the rights secured by those very actions. Perhaps due to her background and experiences, Carena analyzed them with me after venting her frustration. Yet despite having gone through similar struggles, Carena herself had never succumbed to such chaotic numbness, nor was she ever proud of mediocrity or arrogant about aiding oppression; she just chose not to speak of it. She didn’t speak of it. The conversation quickly returned to her research at the time—she was working on a historical trace of ethane research and needed to translate a 19th-century German academic paper. I found a friend in Germany to help her translate it. She half-jokingly mentioned her sapiosexual tendencies and her close bond with another friend working on DNA folding in the lab; she had dyed her own DNA, feeling a sense of joy that transcended sexuality and reproduction. She talked about how she and her lab partner spent their time “doing experiments, discussing, writing papers, grinding through online courses, and cuddling,” finding it far more directly appealing and fulfilling than conventional relationships with explicit sexual intimacy. When our chat ended, I took the key points she had highlighted and continued consulting with my friend about translation details and timelines. That was December. I never imagined that our academic collaboration over the New Year would be our very last.

After the Caixia case, Carena faced continuous threats and harassment. I was already aware of the situation, explained the legal details to her, and told her not to worry. One of the informants knew about Carena’s connection to Caixia and, even after we stated our stance clearly, used fabricated scenarios from the case to intimidate her. But Carena didn’t hide. In late February 2023, the situation suddenly turned critical: one of Carena’s partners (who was an adult at the time) was detained by the police, and her phone was used as bait to trap Carena, bringing up illicit substances they had never bought or used, and crudely inviting her to meet up to use drugs. Carena naturally refused, and only through private communication did we realize her partner had been compromised (the police’s target was crystal clear: they knew the partner wasn’t involved and simply wanted to set a trap across provincial lines to arrest Carena). At the time, I didn’t connect this to the Caixia case, but out of caution, I immediately hired a lawyer for Carena (yes, not just a “suggestion”) and paid for the initial consultation. Carena met with the lawyer after class and signed the retainer, yet within less than a week, she died by suicide on a live stream. Due to the time difference, I did not receive the notification in time to intervene, obtaining only her suicide note and video after the fact. Together with her partner, we took over her QQ account (as her parents had seized her device, logged into her account, deleted her friends, and locked her Qzone).

…It wasn’t until after Carena passed away that we learned she had been named in Caixia’s police statements. A key strategy of the defense at the time was to have everyone named in the statements testify in court that the statements were false; with Carena’s passing, death closed that door, and the strategy fell through. To this day, I still feel that if we had known the contents of those statements earlier, perhaps not only Caixia’s sentence, but Carena’s fate itself, could have been rewritten. Still could have been rewritten. No ODs, no forced psychiatric commitment, no threats from the school or her peers—in one sudden, abrupt moment, Carena left us, so hastily that even her suicide note was too rushed to be suitable for public release.

After Carena passed away, special police moved onto the campus.

I still remember Carena telling me that the status she posted on her Qzone—“Time to fly”—was a precursor to her death. Yet that preview came a year and a half late, at a moment I had never truly anticipated. It was strangely sudden, leaving no time for goodbyes, yet it didn’t feel entirely unexpected. Because that was Carena—that was just who she was. Walking through campus in the drizzling rain of spring, watching endless ripples rise and overlap on the open canal, the tender green leaves of trees, the high, clear glass reflecting a pale grey sky, I couldn’t help but think: she had so candidly shared her persona with me for my gender studies paper, and then, shortly after my paper was completed, she liberated herself from the body she had suffered in, struggled with, yet ultimately accepted—flying freely to wherever she wished. Perhaps she came to the Netherlands, soaring and roaming. No unwanted responsibilities could constrain her anymore, no uncomprehending parents could hold her hostage, and no system in any country could ever discipline, confine, or control her again. Yes, the university refused to acknowledge her death, and the counsellor was extraordinarily callous—fitting every bitter impression she and I had vented about in the past. But so what? So what? Carena had never truly fallen into their hands anyway, just as her life never belonged to us, to her parents, or to anything in this world that presumed to claim her. She was always flying among the clouds, alighting briefly upon the earth to touch our human realm, leaving behind traces like stray feathers shining in the light. I recalled her talking to me about her exploration of her gender identity—how chaotic the process was, yet how decisive the conclusion: she would be queer for life, she was queer for life. The label itself didn’t matter; what mattered was that this was who she was, and this was what she chose. It was one of the few choices in her life where she no longer rebelled, no longer hesitated.

If anyone asks whether I regret everything I did for Carena—the financial aid, the emotional support, the academic collaboration; helping her prepare for exams, fighting for her re-enrollment, standing up to her family, protesting alongside her; hiring a lawyer, settling her debts, and carrying the fallout—my answer is, and will forever be, only one thing: I do not regret it. I didn’t in the past, and I won’t in the future. Because when I help a person, I never demand or force them to repay me. In the end, it was her own life. I was there, I knew her, and I gave everything I could. My past efforts brought her moments of happiness—even if fleeting, instantaneous, and rare in this vast and cold world—and that makes it all worthwhile. I remain deeply grateful for all the moments we shared: the absurd laughter in the dark, cold nights, the blazing sparks of vitality. These things matter. These things matter. As for everything else—everything that battered her, everything that ultimately stole her life away, no amount of absurdity, pain, discrimination, or persecution can ever erase the time we shared and now possess forever. You can take her life, but you cannot kill our existence. My only regret is that I didn’t get to tell her one last time before she died: I don’t blame her. It’s okay. As long as she could be happy, that’s all that mattered. I would handle everything left behind, so please don’t worry.

…And yet I feel she already knew. She must have known. If she ever came to the Netherlands to see everything she had wanted to experience herself, she would probably laugh uncontrollably and ask how I could ever think she didn’t know.

After remembering all of this, I realize that no matter what happened, Carena will always be that person in my mind—grinning at the world with worldly weariness on campus, swinging on the edge of death while looking back with a smile at the rest of us trying our best to survive. Her life could be heavy or light; heavy enough to feel like a stone slab crushing the sky with no room to breathe, or light as a single feather floating away into the light. But a presence like hers—one that invoked resentment, envy, or helplessness in others—was the one thing that would never, ever submit to discipline, to the ideologies built upon that discipline, or to the conventional ethics derived beneath it. Perhaps on the day when transgender identities are fully depathologized in China, when I pay my respects to her, she will appear in my dreams with that same reckless smile, unable to hide her amusement:

“Sis Emma, you still remember all that? Aren’t you tired?”

“Me? Never. I’m not tired at all.”

…A life lived on its own terms, lived indifferently, probably never gets tired. It is left to those of us who do get tired, who carry the weight, who remember, and who hold on, to build these lasting memorials for them—and to look up at those soaring between the clouds and the light.
